\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) historically rely on rigid, lexical keyword matching, frequently discarding highly qualified candidates who lack exact phrase alignments. To resolve this structural inefficiency, we engineered an automated, human-centric resume screening pipeline grounded in deep Natural Language Processing (NLP). We evaluated asymmetric bi-encoder representations—specifically leveraging Sentence-BERT (SBERT) for dense semantic vectorization—against baseline statistical term frequencies (TF-IDF). To mathematically operationalize recruiter intuition regarding tenure, we integrated a non-linear, piecewise Experience Penalty ($\Delta E$). Crucially, to prevent data leakage and ensure rigorous algorithmic generalization, we implemented a group-wise zero-shot evaluation on unseen job descriptions using \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit}. After benchmarking pointwise continuous regression and binary classification models, we transitioned the architecture to a listwise Learning-to-Rank (LTR) framework. Our final gradient-boosted sorting engine, powered by \texttt{LGBMRanker} paired with dense semantic embeddings, achieved a peak Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\text{NDCG}@10$) of $0.9398$. These results confirm that framing recruitment as a relative ranking optimization, rather than an absolute pointwise classification, delivers superior sorting fidelity for high-volume applicant pools.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background Survey and Engineering Relevance}
Conventional recruitment infrastructures exhibit severe algorithmic vulnerabilities by relying almost exclusively on exact lexical overlap. When a technical requisition demands a ``Software Engineer'' but a candidate lists ``Backend Developer,'' boolean search architectures trigger false negatives, artificially suppressing qualified talent. We designed this project to rectify this algorithmic blind spot. We assert that true candidate screening requires contextual understanding, not mere character string matching. By deploying a system that assesses applicant portfolios based on semantic intent rather than superficial syntax, we provide HR professionals with an engine that mirrors human intuition while operating at computational speed.

\section{Core Purpose of the Project}
We sought to move beyond shallow keyword extraction by teaching a machine to interpret professional context. We projected resumes and job descriptions into a shared, high-dimensional semantic vector space. This spatial projection allows our system to evaluate candidate skills contextually. Ultimately, we shifted the foundational recruitment paradigm. We bypassed standard binary ``Accept/Reject'' classification constraints, opting instead to frame candidate screening as a Learning-to-Rank (LTR) problem. This ensures the engine outputs a continuously sorted, probabilistic queue of top-tier talent.

\section{Deployed Technology Stack}
To build, audit, and benchmark this end-to-end processing engine, we utilized the following open-source frameworks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Wrangling:} Python, \href{https://pandas.pydata.org/}{\texttt{pandas}}, and \href{https://numpy.org/}{\texttt{numpy}} for handling complex tabular transformations.

\chapter{Methodology}

This chapter describes the chronological data processing pipeline, feature engineering steps, statistical formulations, and machine learning models used to build our candidate ranking engine.

\section{Data Source and Ingestion}
The empirical foundation of our study relies on an open-source recruitment dataset curated by Neuralframe AI and published by Saugata Roy Arghya on \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/saugataroyarghya/resume-dataset}{Kaggle}. The dataset consists of $9,544$ candidate records stored in a single $17$~MB tabular file (\texttt{resume\_data.csv}) across $35$ structured and unstructured attributes. Distributed under the Creative Commons CC BY-NC 4.0 license, the schema captures detailed candidate profiles including career objectives, skill inventories, educational institutions, employment history, and target job specifications.

\section{Text Cleaning and Linguistic Filtering}
Raw resumes and job descriptions contain significant noise, non-standard formatting, and generic jargon that can obscure real candidate qualifications. To standardize the text before feature extraction, we implemented a central cleaning function in \texttt{utils.py} using \href{https://spacy.io/}{\texttt{spaCy}}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural Sanitization:} Irrelevant metadata fields (such as URLs and certificate expiration dates) were dropped. Missing text entries were filled with empty string placeholders, and location fields were standardized to a uniform ``City, State'' format.
    
    \item \textbf{Text Normalization \& Fluff Removal:} Text was lowercased, stripped of punctuation, and filtered to remove standard English stop-words. Additionally, a custom exclusion list of over $50$ generic HR filler phrases (e.g., ``team player'', ``hardworking professional'') was applied to force embedding models to focus on core technical competencies.
    
    \item \textbf{Length \& Outlier Filtering:} To prevent extreme document lengths from diluting vector embeddings, records with fewer than $20$ words (insufficient detail) or more than $350$ words (excessive noise) were trimmed. After removing exact duplicate pairs, our clean dataset stabilized at $9,369$ records spanning $28$ distinct job postings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feature Engineering}

\subsection{Temporal Experience Calculation and Non-Linear Penalty}

Candidate work history was parsed to calculate total industry experience ($\text{Exp}_{\text{cand}}$) relative to job requirements ($\text{Exp}_{\text{req}}$), defining the experience gap as $\Delta E = \text{Exp}_{\text{cand}} - \text{Exp}_{\text{req}}$. 

To reflect recruiter decision-making—where missing required experience carries an accelerating risk compared to the minor benefit of surplus tenure—a piecewise penalty function was designed:

\begin{equation}
\text{Penalty}(\Delta E) = 
\begin{cases} 
-\alpha \cdot (\Delta E)^2, & \text{if } \Delta E < 0 \quad (\text{Under-qualified}) \\
+\beta \cdot \Delta E, & \text{if } \Delta E \ge 0 \quad (\text{Requirement met})
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

To avoid distorting $[0, 1]$ bounded similarity scores, the weights were empirically calibrated on our training set to \textbf{$\alpha = 0.04$} and \textbf{$\beta = 0.01$}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quadratic Deficit Penalty ($\alpha = 0.04$):} Penalizes under-qualification at an accelerating rate. Missing $2$ years deducts $-0.16$ ($16\%$ score drop), while missing $4$ years incurs a severe $-0.64$ penalty, filtering out severely under-qualified candidates.
    
    \item \textbf{Linear Surplus Reward ($\beta = 0.01$):} Grants a modest boost for extra tenure ($+0.03$ for $3$ extra years), rewarding experience without allowing over-qualified candidates to dominate over technical skill matches.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic Role-Based Context Flags}
To ensure fair evaluation across diverse job types, binary indicators (\texttt{flag\_entry\_level}, \texttt{flag\_academic}, \texttt{flag\_location\_role}) were computed based on job titles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Entry-Level Roles:} Injects academic projects and extracurricular activities into the processed text block.
    \item \textbf{Academic/Research Roles:} Prioritizes publications, certifications, and educational background.
    \item \textbf{Location-Sensitive Roles:} Appends candidate location matching metrics to the text representation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Vector Space Representation and Embeddings}
Candidate-job pairs were projected into three distinct vector spaces to evaluate lexical and semantic similarity:

\subsection{Sparse Statistical Vectors (TF-IDF)}
A \texttt{TfidfVectorizer} from \href{https://scikit-learn.org/}{\texttt{scikit-learn}} extracted the top $8,000$ $n$-grams ($n \in \{1, 2\}$) to compute keyword overlap. Word importance is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{TF-IDF}(i, j) = \text{tf}_{i,j} \times \log\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}_i}\right)
\end{equation}
Keyword similarity ($S_{\text{tfidf}}$) was calculated using Cosine Similarity between candidate vector $\mathbf{u}_{\text{cv}}$ and job vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{jd}}$:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{tfidf}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}_{\text{cv}} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{\text{jd}}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{\text{cv}}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}_{\text{jd}}\|_2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dense Semantic Embeddings (SBERT)}
To capture contextual meaning beyond literal word matches, Sentence-BERT bi-encoders leveraging \href{https://huggingface.co/nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5}{\texttt{nomic-embed-text-v1.5}} mapped complete text blocks into $768$-dimensional dense vectors ($\mathbf{e}_{\text{cv}}, \mathbf{e}_{\text{jd}} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$). Dense similarity was computed as:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{dense}} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_{\text{cv}} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{\text{jd}}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{cv}}\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_{\text{jd}}\|_2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Hybrid OLS Blend}
An Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model was fitted on the training split to blend sparse keyword matches and dense semantic similarity into a unified score:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{hybrid}} = w_0 + w_1 S_{\text{tfidf}} + w_2 S_{\text{dense}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Data Leakage Prevention via Group-Aware Splitting}

Randomly splitting data causes \textbf{data leakage} if resumes applying for the same job description appear in both training and test sets. When this happens, models simply memorize specific job vocabulary rather than learning generalized candidate ranking rules.

To ensure strict evaluation, an $80/20$ group split was implemented using \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.GroupShuffleSplit.html}{\texttt{GroupShuffleSplit}}, grouping records by \texttt{job\_position\_name}. Letting $G_j$ represent all candidates for job $j$, the split guarantees that training and test job postings are completely disjoint:

\begin{equation}
\left( \bigcup_{j \in \text{Train}} G_j \right) \cap \left( \bigcup_{k \in \text{Test}} G_k \right) = \emptyset
\end{equation}

This assigned $7,356$ rows ($22$ jobs) to the training set and $2,013$ rows ($6$ completely unseen jobs) to the test set. All vectorizers, scalers, and models were fitted exclusively on the training split so that test metrics measure true generalization to new job postings.

\section{Multi-Phase Modeling Framework}

Candidate evaluation was structured across three modeling paradigms to compare standard pointwise machine learning techniques against listwise ranking algorithms:

\subsection{Phase 1: Continuous Regression Baselines (Pointwise Scoring)}
The initial phase evaluated standard regression models—including \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression.html}{\texttt{LinearRegression}}, \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.Ridge.html}{\texttt{Ridge}}, \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestRegressor.html}{\texttt{RandomForestRegressor}}, and \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.HistGradientBoostingRegressor.html}{\texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor}}—to predict continuous relevance match scores $y_i \in [0, 1]$. Models were optimized by minimizing Mean Squared Error (MSE):

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Phase 2: Binary Classification Baselines (Threshold Filtering)}
To simulate initial HR screening—where candidates are grouped into ``Shortlisted'' or ``Rejected'' categories—the target variable was binarized at predetermined match thresholds. Classifiers including \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression.html}{\texttt{LogisticRegression}}, \href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestClassifier.html}{\texttt{RandomForestClassifier}}, and Support Vector Classifier (\href{https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.svm.SVC.html}{\texttt{SVC}}) were trained to minimize Binary Cross-Entropy:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ y_i \log(\hat{p}_i) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{p}_i) \right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Phase 3: Learning-to-Rank Framework (LTR)}
To address the limitations of pointwise regression and binary classification, the final phase deployed specialized Learning-to-Rank (LTR) algorithms: \href{https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/python/python_api.html#xgboost.XGBRanker}{\texttt{XGBRanker}} and \href{https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/stable/pythonapi/lightgbm.LGBMRanker.html}{\texttt{LGBMRanker}}. 

Instead of evaluating candidates individually, LTR models ingest query group structures $\mathbf{q}$ defined by \texttt{job\_position\_name}. The models optimize pairwise and listwise ranking losses (such as \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LambdaMART}{LambdaMART}) to directly maximize ranking quality metrics:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Pairwise}} = \sum_{i, j \in \mathbf{q}} \log_2 \left( 1 + e^{-\sigma (s_i - s_j)} \right)
\end{equation}

\section{Model Evaluation Metrics}

To comprehensively assess performance across all three modeling paradigms, evaluation metrics were selected to capture continuous score accuracy, binary classification performance, and listwise candidate ranking quality.

\subsection{Continuous Regression Metrics (Pointwise Accuracy)}
Continuous score predictions were evaluated on the test set using standard regression metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error ($RMSE$):}
    \begin{equation}
    RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):}
    \begin{equation}
    R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Binary Classification Metrics (Screening Quality)}
For threshold-based accept/reject screening, models were evaluated using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Area Under the ROC Curve ($\text{ROC-AUC}$):} Evaluates diagnostic separation ability across thresholds.
    
    \item \textbf{$F_1$-Score:}
    \begin{equation}
    F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Information Retrieval \& Ranking Metrics (Listwise Quality)}
Learning-to-Rank models were evaluated using query-grouped Information Retrieval (IR) metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\text{NDCG}@K$):}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{DCG}@K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \quad \text{NDCG}@K = \frac{\text{DCG}@K}{\text{IDCG}@K}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$):}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_q}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)}

We partitioned the resulting $9,369$ candidate interactions via \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} to guarantee zero-shot evaluation on the test fold.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}

\section{Project Conclusions}
We architected a human-centric, high-velocity screening pipeline to obsolete rigid keyword matching architectures. Our empirical data generation yields three definitive engineering axioms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dense Contextualization Obliterates Lexical Matching:} We proved that transformer-generated embeddings eclipse $n$-gram statistical parsing. Our semantic LTR architecture achieved an $\text{NDCG}@10$ of $0.9398$, proving its ability to detect structural skill equivalencies that keyword matchers ignore.
    \item \textbf{Ranking Paradigm Superiority:} We empirically verified that predicting absolute scores or hard classification boundaries fundamentally contradicts real-world HR triage logic. Shifting to listwise gradient-boosted ranking dynamically solves the core recruitment query.
    \item \textbf{Validation Purity:} We demonstrated that isolating candidate pools via \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} is mathematically mandatory to suppress data leakage and ensure true zero-shot evaluation on future job requests.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations for Future Extensions}
We recommend several probabilistic and interpretability expansions to advance this \texttt{LGBMRanker} backend into a full production system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bayesian Probability Integration:} We suggest reframing the Experience Penalty ($\Delta E$) determinism into a Bayesian inference model, extracting posterior match distributions rather than rigid split constraints.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual Bias Mitigation:} We must investigate stratified counterfactual sampling during the NLP ingestion phase to prevent the algorithm from artificially weighting legacy corporate brand nouns.
    \item \textbf{Generative Justification Output:} We propose overlaying a lightweight Large Language Model (LLM) strictly as a post-ranking justification layer. This would output concise human-readable summaries justifying why the gradient booster ranked specific candidate profiles highly.
\end{itemize}
